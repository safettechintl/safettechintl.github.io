Sublimation vs Cut-and-Sew Football Kits — Which Should Your Club Choose?

Every club that orders a new kit faces the same early decision, and it shapes everything that follows: should the kit be sublimated, or cut-and-sewn? As a custom football kits manufacturer in Sialkot, Pakistan, we make both, and there is no single right answer — only the right answer for your club's design, budget, playing level and deadline. This guide explains both methods plainly, compares them side by side, and ends with a short checklist you can use before you send your design to any manufacturer.

What sublimation actually means

In sublimation, the design is printed onto transfer paper and then heat-pressed into the fabric itself, before the garment is cut and stitched. The ink bonds with the polyester fibres rather than sitting on top of them, so the design becomes part of the cloth. That is why a sublimated kit can carry a full photographic print, a gradient, a camouflage pattern or a sponsor logo wrapped around the side panel — all in one pass, with no extra layer to peel, crack or fade at the edges.

Sublimation works on light-coloured polyester fabrics, which is the standard fabric family for modern football kits. It is the method behind almost every bold, all-over club design you see at grassroots and amateur level today: a club badge, player names and numbers, sponsor marks and an intricate pattern can all be printed in the same process, in unlimited colours, without paying per colour the way older printing methods charged.

What cut-and-sew actually means

Cut-and-sew is the traditional tailoring route. Panels of fabric — each in a solid colour, or carrying a simple printed or woven element — are cut to pattern and stitched together to build the garment. Colour comes from the fabric itself, not from a print: a red body panel, white sleeves and navy side inserts are three separate pieces of cloth joined by seams. Badges, numbers and sponsor marks are then added by embroidery, heat transfer or screen printing, as separate steps after the shirt is made.

This is how most professional match shirts and premium training wear are constructed, and it is the route a club chooses when the design is built from blocks of colour rather than an all-over graphic. It gives a structured, tailored feel, and it allows fabric mixing in a way sublimation does not — a heavier, more premium fabric on the body, a mesh panel under the arm for ventilation, a ribbed collar in a contrasting colour, each chosen for its own job.

Side-by-side comparison

The table below summarises the practical differences, as we see them as a custom football kits manufacturer. Read it against your own design, not in the abstract — a kit with a complex all-over pattern and a kit with two solid colours are different products, and they point to different methods.

• Design freedom: sublimation — unlimited colours, gradients, photos and all-over patterns. Cut-and-sew — strong on solid colour blocks, panels and contrast details; complex graphics cost more because each element is a separate step.

• Print durability: sublimation — the design is in the fabric, so it cannot crack, peel or fade at the edges with washing. Cut-and-sew — the fabric colour itself never fades, but separately applied badges, numbers and transfers need correct heat and pressure to last as long as the shirt.

• Feel and weight: sublimation — no added print layer, so the shirt stays light and breathable. Cut-and-sew — can feel more structured and premium, especially with mixed fabrics, but every added transfer or embroidery adds a little weight.

• Breathability: both can be excellent, provided the base fabric is right. Ask for a lightweight, moisture-wicking polyester for match kits, and mesh ventilation panels where players need them most — under the arms and across the back.

• Reordering: sublimation — reorders match exactly, because the print file is stored and reprinted. Cut-and-sew — reorders depend on the same fabric colours still being available, so keep a record of the exact fabric shades used.

• Lead time: both follow the same sampling rhythm — a sample in 7–10 working days once we have your design or tech file — with bulk time depending on quantity and complexity rather than on the method alone.

Design freedom: where each method wins

If your kit design lives in its artwork — a fading gradient, a tonal pattern across the chest, a large graphic that flows from front to back — sublimation is the only method that reproduces it faithfully. Trying to build that design from sewn panels would mean dozens of tiny pieces, weak seams and a price that makes no sense. Sublimation also suits clubs that change sponsor or add a new badge mid-season: the artwork file is updated and the next batch prints correctly, with no new screens or plates to remake.

Cut-and-sew wins when the design is deliberately simple and the club wants the shirt to feel like a premium garment rather than a printed one. Think of a classic home shirt — solid body, contrasting sleeves, a knitted collar, an embroidered badge. Each element is physically present, not printed, and the shirt reads as tailored from a distance.

Durability, washing and match-day reality

A kit is washed far more often than most garments, and usually in a hurry — after training, after a muddy away match, sometimes by a parent or a kit manager running a full squad load. Sublimated prints survive this well because there is nothing on the surface to lift: wash cold or warm, avoid harsh bleach, and the design stays as printed. Cut-and-sew shirts are equally durable in their fabric, but their applied elements deserve a little care — wash inside out, avoid ironing directly over transfers, and tell whoever does the laundry that embroidered badges can snag if washed with open zips or Velcro.

Neither method is "better" for durability in general; each has a weak point to manage. For sublimation, the weak point is fabric choice — a poor-quality polyester will pill or lose shape long before the print fails, so judge the sample fabric, not just the print. For cut-and-sew, the weak point is application quality — a transfer pressed at the wrong temperature will lift after a few washes, so check the sample's numbers and sponsor marks after washing it, not just when it arrives.

Fit, sizing and the sample you must check

Fit is decided by the pattern, not by the printing method, but the two methods sample differently. A sublimated sample shows you the final artwork in full, so you can judge colours, badge placement and number sizes exactly. A cut-and-sew sample shows you the garment's structure — how the panels sit, how the collar holds its shape, how the shirt moves when a player runs. Either way, do not approve bulk production from photos alone. Order a sample in 7–10 working days, try it on players of different builds, wash it once, and check the size run against your squad list before you confirm quantities.

When you send sizes, give the full run, not just "medium and large". A typical club order mixes youth and adult sizes, goalkeeper kits in different colours, and a few spare shirts for late signings. Our trial MOQ for teamwear is 15–50 pieces per style, which lets a smaller club order a genuine first kit without committing to a full season's stock before the design is proven on the pitch.

Which should your club choose? A short checklist

Work through these questions with your committee or kit manager, and the answer usually becomes clear:

• Does the design use gradients, photos, camouflage or an all-over pattern? If yes, choose sublimation.

• Is the design made of solid colour blocks, with an embroidered badge and a classic collar? If yes, cut-and-sew will look and feel more premium.

• Do you need unlimited colours and sponsor logos included without per-colour charges? Sublimation handles this in one print.

• Do you want mixed fabrics — mesh ventilation, a heavier body cloth, a ribbed collar? Cut-and-sew gives you that freedom.

• Will you reorder the same kit next season with only a sponsor change? Both work, but sublimation reorders match most reliably from a stored print file.

• Is the kit for a one-off tournament, a training range or a full matchday set? A tournament set with a bold design suits sublimation; a matchday set you want to last several seasons suits either, judged on the design above.

If your design sits between the two — a mostly solid shirt with one bold graphic panel, for example — ask the manufacturer. A hybrid is often possible: a sublimated front panel sewn into an otherwise cut-and-sew shirt, or a cut-and-sew body with sublimated sleeves. The right factory will tell you honestly which combination serves the design, rather than pushing the method it prefers to sell.
